The bird, the parts, the market, and how to trade it. Built for a first year on the desk.

Start here

You are selling pieces of a bird that has to balance. Every chicken comes with two breasts, two wings, two legs and a back. A plant cannot make more breast without making more legs and wings, so when one part is hot, another part is cheap. Most of this business is knowing which is which, this week.

The bird

Almost everything traded in the US is a broiler (also called a broiler/fryer): a young meat chicken, about 6 to 8 weeks old at processing. Plants are built around a target live weight, and that weight decides what the plant makes and who buys it.

< 4.25 lb
Small bird

Sold bone-in as 8-piece or 9-piece cut-up. Buyer: fast food (QSR) fried-chicken chains and some foodservice. Small birds make small wings.

4.25 – 6.25 lb
Medium / tray pack

Cut into parts and tray-packed for the grocery meat case, or sold whole for rotisserie. Buyer: retail.

6.25 lb +
Big bird / deboning

Many plants run 8 to 9 lb birds. Deboned into B/S breast, tenders and B/S thigh meat; the wings are jumbo wings. Buyer: further processors, foodservice, retail boneless. This is where the industry is growing.

Live-weight lines vary a little by company. A live bird dresses out to a WOG of roughly 72 to 75 percent of its live weight: a 6.5 lb bird becomes about a 4.7 lb WOG, then gets cut up or deboned.

Other birds you will hear about

  • Cornish game hen: a very young, small bird, around 2 lb or under, sold whole as a single serving.
  • Roaster: an older, heavier bird sold whole. A small market.
  • Fowl / spent hen: a laying hen past her egg years. Tough meat, goes to soup, stock and canned products.
  • Turkey is a separate market with its own quotes, seasons and plants. Same ideas, different bird.

Fresh chicken is perishable and the plant runs every day whether or not it has orders. A plant wants product sold before it is packed. That urgency is why brokers and traders exist: someone has to find a home for what the plant is long on, fast, at a price that still works.

Forms, packs and claims

Fresh

Never frozen. Packed CVP (chill-pack vacuum bags flushed with CO2) or on ice. Shelf life is roughly 10 to 14 days from pack date, so the clock is always running.

Frozen

IQF (individually quick frozen, pieces stay loose) or block/bulk frozen. Good for about a year. Export, foodservice and anything that needs to sit.

Tray pack

Retail-ready trays, packer label or store brand. Medium birds, grocery meat case.

Bulk

40 lb cases (often 4 bags of 10 lb) for foodservice. Combo bins of about 2,000 lb for further processors. 15 kg and 20 kg boxes for export leg quarters and paws.

Grade

USDA Grade A means no bruises, broken bones, tears or missing parts. Plant grade or no grade is ungraded and cheaper. Retail wants A; further processors often do not care.

Claims

NAE / ABF (no antibiotics ever), organic, air-chilled, Halal. Enhanced means solution was added, up to 15 percent. Each claim is a different price and a different buyer.

Sizing

  • WOGs by weight range: 2.5-3, 3-3.5, 3.5-4 lb and up. Rotisserie programs are picky about this.
  • Breast by ounce range (4-6, 6-8, 8-10 oz) or simply "jumbo" from big birds.
  • Wings by size: small, medium, jumbo. Bigger bird, bigger wing. Sold whole (3-joint), 2-joint (tip off) or portioned into drumettes and flats.
  • Thighs and drums by ounce range or by count per bag.

Where the price comes from

Urner Barry (UB, now part of Expana) publishes daily wholesale quotes for each part by region. Everyone still calls it UB, and most trades and contracts are priced off it: "UB flat" is the quote, "UB minus 10" is ten cents a pound under, "UB plus 5" is a nickel over. USDA AMS publishes free public price and volume reports that are worth reading alongside it.

What moves the quote

Supply

Chick placements and egg sets (USDA, weekly) tell you how many birds show up in 6 to 8 weeks. Bird weights, plant outages and bird flu (HPAI) change it fast.

Demand

QSR promotions and retail features move breast and wings in a hurry. Grilling season lifts breast and leg quarters. Football lifts wings.

Exports

Leg quarters and paws. Mexico and China have been the big destinations, and a trade dispute or an HPAI ban shows up in the LQ and paw quotes within days.

Costs and stocks

Corn and soybean meal set the cost of growing a bird. USDA cold storage (monthly) tells you how much frozen chicken is sitting in warehouses waiting to weigh on the market.

Trading 101

A broker or trader sits between the plant and the buyer. The plant is long on something and wants it gone; the buyer needs product at a price and a date. You make the margin between the two, minus freight, and you carry the relationship, the credit risk and sometimes the inventory.

Load math

  • 40 lb case × 50 cases per pallet = 2,000 lb per pallet. About 21 pallets fills a reefer: roughly 42,000 lb.
  • One cent per pound on a 42,000 lb load is $420. A nickel is $2,100. That is why people argue over pennies.
  • A combo bin is about 2,000 lb of bulk product, so a load is about 21 combos.

Every trade needs these nailed down

  1. Spec: the full stack from the example above. Form, cut, size, pack, grade, claims.
  2. Quantity: cases, pounds or loads.
  3. Price basis: a flat number, or UB plus or minus, and on what day's quote.
  4. Freight: FOB plant or delivered, and who arranges the truck.
  5. Dates: pack date, ship date, and days of shelf life on arrival.
  6. Terms: who pays when, and whether credit has been approved.
  7. Confirmation: in writing, same day, before the truck moves.

Red flags

  • A fresh price that looks too good. Ask the pack date. Short-dated product is cheap for a reason.
  • "Jumbo" with no bird size behind it, or "Grade A" from a plant that does not grade.
  • Freight terms nobody said out loud. A delivered price with a 900-mile haul can eat the whole margin.
  • A new buyer in a hurry with no credit on file.

How to get good at this

The traders who last are not the loudest ones. They know the market before they pick up the phone, they do what they said they would do, and they stay curious about every plant, every buyer and every load.

Every morning, before the first call (15 minutes)

  • Read the UB (Expana) chicken report and the USDA daily. Note what moved and by how much.
  • Log five prices in a notebook or sheet: B/S breast, tenders, jumbo wings, leg quarters, WOGs. A month of this and you can feel the market.
  • Check anything you have in transit: truck location, delivery appointment, any temperature or date issue.
  • Call three people with no agenda: a plant, a buyer, a trucker. "What are you long on?" "What's moving?" "Anything short-dated?"

Habits that compound

  1. Know the spec cold. Be able to describe any item by form, cut, size, pack, grade, claims and dates without looking. Quiz tab, every day, until it is boring.
  2. Build a plant sheet. For every plant you deal with: bird size, what they debone, pack styles, who to call, when they run short weeks. Nobody will hand you this; you build it one call at a time.
  3. Keep a trade log. Every trade, win or lose: buyer, seller, spec, price versus UB, freight, margin, and one line on what you would do differently.
  4. Learn freight. Reefer rates on your main lanes, pallet counts, cold storage costs, what a late truck costs. Freight is where margins quietly die.
  5. Go see it. Walk a plant floor and a further processor. Once you have watched a deboning line, "jumbo" and "woody breast" stop being words.
  6. Call back when you said you would. This business runs on the phone and on memory. People trade with the ones who are easy to reach and never surprise them.
  7. Your word is the contract. A verbal trade is a trade. If you say a load is sold, it is sold. Reputation is the only thing you own.
  8. Think in loads and cents. Every time you hear a price, do the load math in your head. It keeps you honest about what a deal is really worth.

Questions to ask Dad this week

  • Which plants do we buy from most, and what size bird does each one run?
  • When you quote UB minus on a load, how do you decide the minus?
  • Who do you call first when a buyer cancels and we have a load on the road?
  • How do we decide who gets credit, and how much?
  • What are our three best freight lanes, and what do they cost right now?
  • What was the worst trade you made in your first year, and what did it teach you?

Your first 30 days

Week 1 · Parts and lingo

Bird tab every day. Run the Parts and Lingo quizzes until you score 10 of 10 three times in a row.

Week 2 · Specs

Pull ten real items off our invoices or quote sheets and write out what every word means. Ask about the ones you cannot explain.

Week 3 · The market

Log prices every morning. Each day, explain one price move to Dad in two sentences, and ask whether you got it right.

Week 4 · Trades

Sit in on five calls. Write each one up in the trade log. By the end of the week, quote one load yourself with Dad listening.
